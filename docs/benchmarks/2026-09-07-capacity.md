# Capacity sweep — 2026-09-07

**Machine:** Apple M4, macOS 26.5.2
**Renderer:** ANGLE (Apple, ANGLE Metal Renderer: Apple M4)
**Browser:** Chromium 153, headed (headless has no GPU here — see below)
**Server:** FastAPI on localhost, pre-shuffled binary chunks of 250,000 points
**Canvas:** ~1440 × 420 CSS pixels

Reproduce:

```bash
npx playwright test --config=bench.config.ts     # both pages, writes capacity-latest.md
```

or open `http://localhost:5173/bench.html` (sweep) and
`http://localhost:5173/diag.html` (render diagnostics) by hand.

`fps` is frames deck.gl actually drew during a three-second scripted pan,
divided by that window; `frames` is the raw count behind it. `fps p5` is the
worst-5% frame rate, reported only where at least 30 frames were drawn — below
that a percentile describes the sampling, not the renderer.

Up to half a million points the plot runs at the display's refresh rate. At one
million it holds a steady 30 fps — still smooth to pan and zoom, and note the
p5 of 56.8: individual frames are fast, the renderer is simply producing half as
many of them. Two million is where it becomes visibly heavy.

## The finding that mattered: blending

The first version of this table showed 12.7 fps at one million points, which is
far below what deck.gl should manage on an M4. `diag.html` isolates the cause by
building deck.gl directly, one configuration at a time, at one million points:

| variant | fps |
|---|---:|
| one layer, no picking | 11.7 |
| one layer per 250k chunk | 12.0 |
| chunked + pickable | 10.3 |
| chunked, `useDevicePixels: false` | 11.7 |
| **chunked, blending off** | **38.0** |
| no picking, CSS pixels, no blending | 40.0 |

Chunking and device-pixel ratio cost nothing measurable; picking costs a little.
**Alpha blending costs more than three times the frame rate.** Apple Silicon is a
tile-based deferred renderer, and blending a million mutually overlapping points defeats
the hidden-surface removal that makes that architecture fast.

Blending was only there for two reasons: antialiased point edges, and dimming
unselected cells during a selection. Neither needs it:

- The selection highlight now **darkens the RGB of unselected cells toward the
  background** instead of lowering their alpha, so every point stays opaque.
  Colours are recomputed from the source column on each selection change, so
  repeated selections never compound the dimming (there is a test for exactly
  that).
- Antialiasing at a one-pixel radius is not worth 3× the frame budget.

Translucency remains available as a **"Translucent points"** toggle in the side
panel, because it genuinely does show density in crowded regions. It is off by
default.

Effect of the change, same machine, same harness:

| points | fps before | fps after |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 500,000 | 31.0 | 60.7 |
| 1,000,000 | 12.7 | 30.7 |
| 2,000,000 | 7.3 | 18.0 |
| 5,000,000 | 3.0 | 6.7 |
| 10,000,000 | 2.7 | 5.0 |

`DEFAULT_POINT_BUDGET` in `ScatterCanvas.ts` is set to **1,000,000** on the
strength of that: the largest round number this machine still pans smoothly at.
Half a million would buy the full refresh rate; one million trades that for
showing every cell of a published-scale UMAP at once, which is the point of the
tool. Lower it on a weaker GPU.

## What the other columns say

**The server is not the constraint.** Ten million cells — 40 chunks, 192 MB on
disk — transfer and decode in well under a second from localhost (628 ms here,
260 ms on a warmer run). Decode is near-zero by construction: the browser does `new Float32Array(buffer)` and hands the
result to the GPU, with no parsing step at any size.

**Selection stays interactive far past the point rendering does.** A 60-vertex
lasso over the whole dataset takes 27 ms at one million cells and 363 ms at ten
million, and it runs in a Web Worker, so the main thread is free throughout.
Building the grid index costs 3–119 ms across the whole range.

**Memory is linear and predictable** — roughly 96 bytes per cell in practice
(24 bytes of column data plus the RGBA colour buffer, the grid index and
allocator overhead): 54 MB at one million, 964 MB at ten million. Ten million is
where a 16 GB machine starts to care.

**Overdraw still matters, but secondarily.** Holding point count at ten million
and changing only radius: 0.5 px gives 7.3 fps, 2.5 px gives 4.0 fps. A 25×
increase in drawn area costs 1.8×. Frame cost tracks point *count* far more
than point *area*, which is why the level-of-detail policy caps how many points
are drawn rather than shrinking them.

Because the data is stored pre-shuffled, drawing a million of ten million is an
unbiased uniform sample, so the plot stays structurally honest while
interaction stays smooth. Zooming in raises the fraction, since a narrower
viewport holds fewer points at the same cost.

## Three measurement traps, recorded because each produced wrong numbers first

**Frames must be counted from the renderer, not from `requestAnimationFrame`.**
The first harness drove the camera from an rAF loop and timed its own
callbacks. Those fire whether or not deck.gl draws anything, so it reported
12 fps at one million points during a window in which deck.gl's own
`framesRedrawn` counter said zero.

**A deck.gl transition needs a controller *and* a view-state handler.** The
diagnostics page reported an identical 0.7 fps for all six variants twice in a
row — two frames each, the endpoints of a transition that never animated. The
transition manager lives inside the controller, and in controlled mode it
publishes interpolated states through `onViewStateChange`; a handler that does
not feed them back leaves the camera parked.

**Percentiles need samples.** With eight frames in three seconds, the median
delta is whatever two back-to-back renders happened to do. An earlier run
published 61 fps for ten million points on that basis. The table now prints the
frame count beside the rate and suppresses percentiles below 30 frames.

## Headless is not a measurement environment

Headless Chromium here has no GPU and falls back to SwiftShader, rasterising
every point on the CPU. Measured cost of a three-event drag:

| points | drag round trip |
|---:|---:|
| 100,000 | 3.3 s |
| 1,000,000 | 11.4 s |

That is why the Playwright correctness suite runs its interaction tests against
the 100k dataset — it tests behaviour, not throughput — while performance is
measured only here, headed, on the real GPU.

## Practical guidance

| dataset size | what to expect |
|---|---|
| up to 500k | full refresh rate, nothing to think about |
| 1M (a published UMAP, e.g. Nicheformer's Figure 1 at 1,108,759 cells) | steady 30 fps at full detail, sub-second load, every cell selectable |
| 2M | 18 fps at full detail; auto detail draws a million and restores 30 |
| 5M | auto detail throughout; lasso around a quarter-second |
| 10M | loads in well under a second and selects in ~360 ms; rendering needs the detail budget, and memory is ~1 GB |

Selection, metadata and the chat context always operate on **every loaded
cell**, never on the drawn subsample — level of detail affects what is painted,
not what is analysed.
